## A Connected Abstract: Covenant Headroom and Investment

Debt contracts can influence corporate investment before firms formally violate a covenant. This paper examines whether shrinking financial-covenant headroom is associated with firms' investment decisions and whether financing flexibility explains the response. Using a synthetic panel of 2,400 nonfinancial firms with quarterly covenant thresholds and accounting data, I compare subsequent capital expenditures across firms with different covenant headroom while controlling for observable firm conditions. Firms in the lowest headroom quintile invest 1.2 percentage points less of beginning-of-year assets over the following year than firms with ample headroom. The association is concentrated among firms with large near-term refinancing needs and limited cash reserves. These patterns place debt-contract constraints inside the period of contractual compliance, when financing choices may already respond to the prospect of a future violation.

## Introduction: From Economic Tension to Main Evidence

Financial covenants give lenders contractual protection against deterioration in borrowers' financial condition. The relevant economic margin may emerge before a technical violation: as headroom narrows, managers can preserve covenant capacity by reducing investment or adjusting financing. This possibility connects creditor control to the timing of real decisions. The paper asks whether investment responds to diminishing covenant flexibility while firms remain contractually compliant.

I link quarterly covenant headroom to subsequent investment for an illustrative panel of U.S. nonfinancial firms. The central comparison relates firms' remaining covenant capacity to capital spending over the following four quarters. Firms near the threshold invest 1.2 percentage points less of beginning-of-year assets than firms with ample headroom, conditional on firm and time effects and observable balance-sheet conditions. The difference is larger when debt matures soon and cash reserves are low. Together, these results connect a contractual balance-sheet constraint to the firm's real investment policy and identify refinancing exposure as an economically relevant margin.

## Results: Interpret the Main Comparison

Table 3 relates beginning-of-quarter covenant headroom to investment accumulated over the following four quarters. Firms in the lowest headroom quintile invest 1.2 percentage points less of beginning-of-year assets than firms in the highest quintile, after conditioning on firm characteristics and common time effects. The illustrative 95% confidence interval is 0.6 to 1.8 percentage points for the magnitude of the difference. Against a 6.3% average investment rate, the estimate corresponds to about 19% of annual capital spending. Covenant proximity is therefore economically consequential even among firms that have yet to cross a contractual threshold.

The pattern becomes more pronounced among firms with debt maturing within the next year: their estimated investment gap is 1.8 percentage points, compared with 0.5 percentage points among firms without near-term maturities. This heterogeneity is consistent with managers preserving financing flexibility when refinancing needs make future covenant compliance more valuable. The evidence ties the investment association to a measurable financing constraint and motivates a mechanism interpretation within the limits of the observational comparison.

## Economic Puzzle and Discriminating Evidence: Bank Credit Supply

A bank losing deposits can reduce lending because its funding capacity deteriorates, while simultaneous economic weakness can reduce borrowers' demand for credit. The distinction matters for understanding whether uninsured-deposit fragility transmits into the supply of corporate financing. I compare lending by different banks to the same borrower in the same quarter in an illustrative matched bank–firm panel. Banks with a 10-percentage-point larger uninsured-deposit outflow reduce their lending to that borrower by 6% more than other lenders serving the same firm. The relationship is weaker for banks with larger holdings of immediately liquid securities. The within-borrower comparison isolates differences in bank funding exposure from changes in the borrower's common credit demand, and the liquidity interaction identifies a balance-sheet margin consistent with supply transmission. The result links deposit fragility to credit allocation across banks financing the same firm.
